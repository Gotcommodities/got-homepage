Why Helium Matters

Helium is the only commodity on Earth that is truly non-renewable and non-substitutable for its critical applications. Once released into the atmosphere, helium is too light to be recaptured — it escapes into space. There is no synthetic way to produce it economically; commercial helium comes from radioactive decay trapped in natural gas reservoirs over millions of years. And for its most important uses — cooling MRI superconducting magnets, purging semiconductor fabrication cleanrooms, testing rocket engines, and enabling quantum computing — there is no alternative gas or technology.

The US Federal Helium Reserve near Amarillo, Texas, once held the world's largest helium stockpile (over 30 billion cubic feet at peak). After decades of sales mandated by the Helium Privatization Act of 1996, the reserve is now largely depleted, and the Bureau of Land Management completed its divestiture. This has shifted supply toward private producers and new international sources. Qatar (RasGas/QatarEnergy) is now the world's largest helium supplier, followed by Algeria, Russia, and the US.

The helium market has experienced four major shortages since 2006 (Helium 1.0 through 4.0), each caused by plant outages or geopolitical disruptions. The latest shortage was triggered by the Russia-Ukraine war (Gazprom's Amur plant offline) and Qatar plant maintenance. Demand growth from semiconductor fabrication, space launch cadence, and MRI installations in developing countries is running ahead of new supply capacity. The planned Amur Gas Processing Plant in Russia and new LNG-linked helium plants in Qatar are the only major capacity additions, making the market structurally vulnerable to outages.
