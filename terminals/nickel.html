Why Nickel Matters

Nickel is being reshaped by two forces: Indonesia's explosive supply growth and the EV battery revolution. Indonesia now produces over half of the world's nickel, having leveraged a raw ore export ban (first imposed in 2020) to force investment in domestic smelting and processing. Chinese-Indonesian joint ventures have flooded the market with nickel pig iron (NPI) and, increasingly, battery-grade nickel matte, crashing LME prices from $30,000/t in early 2023 to around $16,000/t.

The traditional nickel market is split between Class 1 (refined metal, suitable for batteries) and Class 2 (ferronickel and NPI, used in stainless steel). Stainless steel still accounts for roughly 70% of nickel demand, but the battery segment is the growth driver. NMC cathodes (nickel-manganese-cobalt) in premium EVs use 30-80 kg of nickel per vehicle. The push toward nickel-rich chemistries (NMC 811) has increased nickel intensity per kWh of battery capacity.

The environmental cost of Indonesian nickel expansion is drawing scrutiny. HPAL (high-pressure acid leaching) plants processing laterite ore generate massive volumes of waste tailings, often disposed in deep-sea or land-based facilities. Carbon intensity is high — Indonesian NPI smelters run on coal power. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) and automaker ESG requirements are creating a two-tier market: premium-priced "green" nickel from established producers like Vale, BHP, and Nornickel versus cheaper Indonesian material with a higher environmental footprint.
