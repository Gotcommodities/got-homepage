03 — WHY IT MATTERS

The Hormuz–LNG Cascade

Qatar is the world's largest LNG exporter, shipping approximately 106 million tonnes per year — virtually all of it through the Strait of Hormuz. Any disruption to Hormuz transit directly threatens Asia's and Europe's gas supply, making LNG one of the most geopolitically sensitive energy commodities on the planet.

The JKM (Japan Korea Marker) Asian spot price has surged to approximately $29 per MMBtu, up roughly 10% on the week, as the Hormuz crisis intensifies. European TTF trades near €28.9 per MWh. The widening premium between Asian spot and US Henry Hub ($3.10) reflects the structural disconnect: US gas remains abundant from shale production, while global spot markets are tightening on supply fears.

The cascade effect is critical: when LNG prices spike, Asian and European utilities switch back to coal, pushing Newcastle thermal coal to near three-year highs. This substitution chain — Hormuz → LNG disruption → coal demand → emissions reversal — is one of the most consequential energy dynamics in the current crisis.

Key Consumers

Japan, China, South Korea, India, and the EU are the top LNG importers. Japan alone imports approximately 70 million tonnes per year, with limited domestic gas production. European imports surged after cutting Russian pipeline gas in 2022.

Chokepoint

The Strait of Hormuz is the single-point-of-failure for Qatari LNG. There is no pipeline bypass for LNG — it must transit by tanker through the 21-mile-wide strait. Roughly 20% of global LNG supply passes through Hormuz daily.
