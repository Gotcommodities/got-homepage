Why Rare Earths Matters

Rare earth elements are the most geopolitically concentrated critical minerals on Earth. While the 17 elements are not geologically rare, economical deposits and processing capacity are overwhelmingly concentrated in China, which controls approximately 60% of global mining and 90% of refining and processing. This gives Beijing extraordinary leverage — demonstrated through export controls on gallium, germanium, and antimony in 2023-2025, with rare earths always the implicit threat.

The key commercial rare earths are neodymium and praseodymium (NdPr), used to make the world's strongest permanent magnets. Every EV motor contains 1-2 kg of NdPr magnets, every offshore wind turbine uses 600+ kg, and precision-guided missiles, fighter jet engines, and submarine motors all depend on them. Dysprosium and terbium — heavy rare earths almost exclusively processed in China — are added to magnets for high-temperature stability in military and automotive applications.

Western nations are racing to build alternative supply chains. MP Materials operates the Mountain Pass mine in California, Lynas Rare Earths processes in Malaysia and is building a US facility, and new projects in Canada, Australia, and Brazil are advancing. But separation and processing — the real bottleneck — takes 5-10 years to build. China's December 2023 ban on rare earth processing technology exports has made this harder. The market is structurally tight for heavy rare earths, and any escalation in US-China trade tensions could trigger supply disruptions overnight.
