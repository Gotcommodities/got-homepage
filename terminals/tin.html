Why Tin Matters

Tin is the invisible backbone of the digital economy. Every semiconductor, circuit board, and electronic connection relies on tin-based solder — the material that bonds components together. With global semiconductor demand growing 8-10% annually driven by AI, EVs, and IoT devices, tin consumption is rising in lockstep. Unlike most base metals, tin has no viable substitute in electronics soldering at scale.

Supply is concentrated and fragile. China, Indonesia, and Myanmar account for approximately 70% of global mine production. Myanmar's Wa State — which produces roughly 10% of global tin — has experienced repeated mine shutdowns due to ethnic militia conflicts and Chinese political pressure. Indonesia, the world's largest exporter, periodically restricts exports and faces depleting onshore reserves. These supply disruptions create chronic price volatility — tin has been the most volatile LME metal in recent years.

The market is structurally tight. Above-ground tin inventories sit at historically low levels, and LME warehouse stocks have fallen below 5,000 tonnes multiple times. New mine development is limited — tin deposits are small and geographically scattered, making large-scale investment unattractive. The combination of growing electronics demand, constrained supply, and low inventories suggests tin will remain one of the tightest and most volatile base metal markets through the decade.
