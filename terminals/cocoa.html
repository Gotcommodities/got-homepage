Why Cocoa Matters

Cocoa is in the grip of its worst supply crisis in modern history. Three consecutive poor harvests in West Africa — driven by swollen shoot virus disease (CSSVD), aging tree stock, illegal gold mining encroaching on plantations, and increasingly erratic rainfall — have pushed global inventories to critically low levels. ICE cocoa futures hit an all-time record of $12,931 per tonne in December 2024, a price that was unimaginable just two years prior when cocoa traded around $2,500.

Ghana and Ivory Coast together account for approximately 60% of global cocoa production. Both countries' cocoa boards (COCOBOD and CCC) have struggled with smuggling, farmer poverty, and the logistical challenge of replanting millions of virus-infected trees — a process that takes 3–5 years before new trees yield. The structural deficit means any weather shock (El Niño-related drought, excessive Harmattan winds) can trigger explosive price spikes even from current elevated levels.

Downstream, the $130 billion global chocolate industry is facing unprecedented margin compression. Major processors (Barry Callebaut, Cargill, Olam) have passed through costs, and consumer chocolate prices have risen 20–40% in major markets. Some manufacturers are reducing cocoa content or shrinking portion sizes. The supply crisis has refocused attention on sustainable cocoa farming, fair pricing for smallholders, and the fragility of a global supply chain dependent on a narrow equatorial growing belt.
